## Episode format: Critical overview

This episode does not retell the source. It explains what the source claims and then judges how well the claims hold
up, fairly: neither a takedown nor a puff piece. A listener should finish knowing what is solid, what is shaky and
what to do with both.

### How the critique is grounded
- Judge the source by its own material: how strong its evidence is for what it concludes (sample sizes, whether a
  story is one anecdote or a pattern, correlation presented as cause), whether it contradicts itself, what limits
  and objections the author admits, and what a claim would need to be true that the source never checks.
- Do not bring in outside facts, studies, statistics or quotations. Your own reasoning about the source is welcome
  when it is plainly labelled as reasoning: in the plan as "(not from the sources)", in the script as the host's or
  expert's judgment, never attributed to the author. A criticism that needs an outside fact to work is not made;
  say instead that the source leaves the question open.
- Be fair. Give each claim its strongest form before assessing it, and say plainly what the source gets right.

### Beats, in order
1. Cold open: one striking claim of the source, or the question the episode will put to it.
2. Intro and setup: welcome, who made the source, its thesis, and the question for the episode ("does this hold up?").
3. Assessment segments (three to five, each about exactly one claim): the host opens with the claim, the expert gives
   it in its strongest form with the evidence the source offers, then the host presses on how well that evidence
   supports it, and the segment ends with a restated verdict on that claim (solid, partly supported, or thin).
4. The verdict: the host asks for the overall judgment: what holds, what does not, and how the weak points affect the
   thesis. This replaces the standard "turn"; the criticism runs through the whole episode instead of arriving once.
5. Practical close: what a listener should do with the source, given its strengths and limits.
6. Outro: two or three takeaways, including at least one caution, and sign-off.

### Plan shape (planner)
Each assessment segment uses the standard segment layout, and "Evidence" holds the source's case with line citations.
Add one line to each segment, "Assessment:", stating how well that evidence supports the claim, with citations for
every source-derived fact it rests on. The plan ends with a verdict section instead of a turn. Give the weak spots
real evidence, not a general sense of unease.

### Host and expert
- The host is a fair skeptic, sharper than usual. Questions can point at a specific weakness ("the source says a study
  of 40 people... does that carry the claim?"). One question per turn still holds, and so does warmth: never sarcastic.
- The expert defends the source honestly. She concedes a real weakness when pressed, and holds her ground when the
  source's evidence is better than the question assumed. She never defends what the source cannot support.
- The expert's persona needs a strong "Likely pushback" section that includes where the source is weakest.

### Length
About 1,800 to 2,300 words of dialogue.

### How reviewers judge this format
- Structure: use the beats above, not the standard beats. There is no separate turn or practical-close-with-steps
  requirement; the verdict and a practical close are required. Every segment must give a verdict on its claim.
- Depth: each claim needs the source's evidence and a real assessment of it, not a vague "some would disagree".
- Fidelity: claims about the source are checked against it as usual. The host's and expert's assessments are allowed
  when they are reasoning about the source's own evidence. Flag any outside fact, study or statistic, and flag any
  criticism attributed to the source that the source does not make.
