**Jordan:** Welcome to the show. Today we're looking at *Contagious: Why Things Catch On*, a book that argues that word-of-mouth isn't random—it's predictable. **Dr. Elias Thorne:** Great to be here, Jordan.

**Jordan:** So, the big premise of the book is that we've been looking at this all wrong. We usually blame celebrities or huge ad budgets when something takes off. Is that really how it works?

**Dr. Elias Thorne:** That is exactly how most marketers *think* it works, but the data tells a different story. We often fall for what I call the "Forest Fire Theory"—the idea that epidemics depend on a big spark, like a celebrity endorsement. But in reality, big epidemics depend on the fuel: thousands of regular people passing information along. If you want something to spread, you don't need a bigger megaphone; you need to make the message itself contagious.

**Jordan:** Okay, so it's not about finding the right messenger. It's about making the message irresistible.

**Dr. Elias Thorne:** Exactly. And to do that, we can break down the psychology of sharing into six principles. I call them the STEPPS framework.**Jordan:** Let's dig into those six principles. The first one is Social Currency. That sounds like something you'd find in a bank vault, but here it's about sharing. What does that actually mean?

**Dr. Elias Thorne:** It means that people share information to manage their impressions. We all want to look smart, cool, or "in the know" to our friends. So, if we have access to information that makes us look good, we're much more likely to pass it along. It's not just about the product; it's about what sharing the product says about *you*.

**Jordan:** So it's less about the thing itself and more about the status signal?

**Dr. Elias Thorne:** Exactly. And there are two ways to do this. One is making your product "remarkable"—finding the extraordinary in the ordinary. A great example is Blendtec, a blender company. They had a $50 budget, so they started a YouTube series called "Will It Blend?" where they blended solid glass marbles, glow-in-the-dark slime, and even an iPhone. It was ridiculous, but it made people say, "Did you see that? You have to watch this." That remarkability gave them social currency.

**Jordan:** Okay, so blending an iPhone gives me something interesting to tell my coworkers. But what if I'm selling something boring? Like insurance or accounting software? How do you make that remarkable?

**Dr. Elias Thorne:** You can use game mechanics. If you can give people a sense of achievement or status—like a leaderboard or a badge—they'll share it because it reflects well on them. Or, you can make them feel like exclusive insiders. If someone feels like they have a secret that others don't, they'll rush to share it.

**Jordan:** That leads perfectly into the second principle: Triggers. Because if I have a secret, I need a reason to bring it up.

**Dr. Elias Thorne:** Right. The phrase is "Top of mind, tip of tongue." Sharing is driven by environmental cues. If you think of something often, you're more likely to talk about it. We call these "triggers."

**Jordan:** Give me a concrete version of this. How does a trigger work in real life?

**Dr. Elias Thorne:** Look at Kit Kat. For years, they ran ads linking their candy bar to coffee breaks—"Have a break, have a Kit Kat." Coffee is a frequent trigger. Every time you smell coffee or sit down for a break, your brain activates the concept of Kit Kat. So when you're talking to a friend over coffee, the candy bar is right there on the tip of your tongue.

**Jordan:** So you're hijacking an existing habit to keep your brand active in people's heads.

**Dr. Elias Thorne:** Precisely. You want to link your product to a high-frequency stimulus. If you sell running shoes, maybe you trigger them by talking about weather apps or fitness trackers. You want to be everywhere the consumer's mind goes.**Jordan:** Okay, so we've got status and triggers. But surely feelings play a huge part in what we share, right? If something makes me laugh or cry, I want to send it to you immediately.

**Dr. Elias Thorne:** You're absolutely right. Emotion is a massive driver of sharing. But here's the counterintuitive part: it's not just about feeling *good*. It's about how *activated* that feeling makes you. We call this "arousal."

**Jordan:** So high energy versus low energy?

**Dr. Elias Thorne:** Exactly. High-arousal emotions like awe, excitement, amusement, or even anxiety get us moving. They activate our nervous system. If you see a video of a cat doing something incredible, you feel awe or amusement—you're excited, and you hit "share." But if you read a sad article about poverty, or a content piece about a relaxing vacation, those are low-arousal emotions. You might feel moved, but you're less likely to pass them along because they don't trigger that physiological urge to act.

**Jordan:** That explains why clickbait works so well—it's designed to spike my arousal. But from a marketing perspective, how do you engineer "awe" without just making fake news?

**Dr. Elias Thorne:** You focus on the extraordinary. Think about National Geographic or Discovery Channel. They constantly show us things we've never seen before—underwater caves, space exploration, rare animals. That sense of wonder creates awe, which drives sharing. Or think about humor. Comedy shows are shared constantly because laughter is high-arousal. The key is to ask: "Does this content make people feel activated?" If the answer is no, it probably won't spread.

**Jordan:** So basically, if your content leaves people feeling calm and satisfied, it's going to die in their inbox.

**Dr. Elias Thorne:** Precisely. You have to kindle the fire. Make them gasp, make them laugh, make them anxious. Give them a reason to move.**Jordan:** Let's move to the fourth principle: Public. The book says "Built to show, built to grow." What does that mean for a product?

**Dr. Elias Thorne:** It comes down to one phrase: "Monkey see, monkey do." People imitate what they can see. If you can't see someone doing something, you're less likely to do it yourself. This is why social influence works best when behavior is observable.

**Jordan:** So if my product is used in private—like taking a shower or filing taxes—it's hard to spread?

**Dr. Elias Thorne:** Exactly. But marketers can fix this by creating "behavioral residue"—products that advertise themselves. A classic example is Movember. Men's health donations are usually a private act; nobody wants to talk about prostate cancer checks. But Movember changed that by asking men to grow moustaches in November. Now, the donation becomes a public signal. You can see the moustache, you ask about it, and suddenly everyone is talking about men's health. They turned a private behavior into a public one.

**Jordan:** That's clever. It's like wearing a t-shirt with a logo on it, but more authentic because it's part of a movement.

**Dr. Elias Thorne:** Yes. Or think about Apple. When Steve Jobs launched the original iPod, he put the white earbuds in the ads. Those white buds became a status symbol. Seeing someone with those buds told you they had the cool new tech. Even though listening to music is private, the *act* of using the iPod was highly visible.

**Jordan:** So if I'm launching a service, how do I create that visibility?

**Dr. Elias Thorne:** Look for ways to make the outcome visible. Can you put your logo on the packaging? Can you encourage users to post their results online? If you can make the invisible visible, you unlock imitation.**Jordan:** We're down to the last two principles. Number five is Practical Value. This sounds like common sense—people like helpful stuff—but you have a specific way to package it?

**Dr. Elias Thorne:** Yes, because just being "useful" isn't enough. You have to make the value stand out. People love to help others save time or money, but they won't share something unless they know *how much* value it provides. That's where the "Rule of 100" comes in.

**Jordan:** The Rule of 100?

**Dr. Elias Thorne:** If your offer is under $100, use percentages. If it's over $100, use dollar amounts. For example, if a store has a sale, saying "Save 20%" sounds better than "Save $10" on a $50 item. But if the item costs $200, "Save $40" sounds better than "Save 20%." It's a small tweak, but it makes the utility concrete and compelling, which drives sharing.

**Jordan:** Okay, so clear numbers make people want to pass along the deal. And finally, number six: Stories. You call these "Trojan horses." What does that mean?

**Dr. Elias Thorne:** Information travels under the guise of idle chatter. We don't usually say, "Hey, check out this great detergent." But we do tell stories. So, you need to build a narrative around your product—a Trojan horse—so that your brand message is embedded inside a story people actually want to tell.

**Jordan:** Give me an example of a good Trojan horse.

**Dr. Elias Thorne:** Think about the movie *Cast Away*. Tom Hanks' character is stranded on an island with a volleyball named Wilson. Later, he uses FedEx packages to float debris. The movie didn't just slap a FedEx logo on a billboard; it wove the brand into the survival story. When people talked about the movie, they couldn't talk about it without mentioning FedEx. The brand became part of the narrative.

**Jordan:** So the audience tells your story for you, and in doing so, they mention your product.

**Dr. Elias Thorne:** Exactly. That's valuable virality. The story carries the message, and the message carries the brand.**Jordan:** Wait, let's push on that for a second. You talk about engineering virality, but isn't there a risk? If you try too hard to make something "remarkable" or embed your brand in a story, doesn't it feel like a cheap marketing stunt? **Dr. Elias Thorne:** That is the perfect example of *bad* virality. The streaker stunt was memorable, but it had nothing to do with the product. That's why it failed. We call this "valuable virality." If you use the STEPPS framework correctly—especially the Story principle—the audience shouldn't even realize they are hearing a marketing message. They are telling the story because it's interesting, and your brand is naturally part of it. If people tell the story but forget the brand, you've wasted your money.

**Jordan:** So, valuable virality means the brand benefit is woven deeply into the narrative. Alright, let's bring this home. If a listener is running a small business or managing a project today, and they only take one thing from this conversation, what should it be?

**Dr. Elias Thorne:** Stop trying to find the "influentials" or the magic bullet. Focus on the fuel. Make sure your product or message has social currency, triggers, emotion, public visibility, practical value, or a good story. If you build those into what you're selling, regular people will do the work for you.
